What's in your coffee, and how a camera can tell

Coffee powder looks the same whether it is pure arabica, cheap robusta or a third chicory. This page explains the ingredients, what mixing does to the cup, and how hyperspectral imaging reads the chemistry to tell them apart.

1

Coffee and what gets mixed into it

Ground coffee is rarely one thing. Most packs are a blend of two coffee species, and many South Indian filter coffees also contain roasted chicory root. Blends are legitimate when they are declared. They become adulteration when a cheaper ingredient is added without saying so, or in larger amounts than the label claims.

Arabica

Coffea arabica

The higher-grown, more delicate species. It makes a sweeter, more aromatic and more acidic cup, and sells for roughly twice the price of robusta.

Other adulterants reported in ground coffee

Chicory and robusta are the common ones in India. Studies around the world also report cheaper fillers that roast to a coffee-like brown powder:

roasted barleycornwheatsoybeancoffee husks and sticksdate seedscaramelised sugar

Cereal fillers also carry a health risk: barley and wheat contain gluten, which is unsafe for people with coeliac disease when it is not declared.

How the adulterants change pure coffee

PropertyAdding chicory to coffeeAdding robusta to arabica
Caffeine↓Diluted: chicory has none, so 30 % chicory means about 30 % less caffeine.↑Robusta has nearly twice the caffeine.
Aroma & acidity↓Fewer coffee aroma compounds per cup; a flatter taste.↓Less of arabica's sweetness and bright acidity.
Body & colour↑Darker, thicker brew from caramelised inulin; why filter coffee carries milk so well.↑Heavier body and more crema.
Bitterness↑Roasted, burnt-sugar bitterness.↑A harsher, more bitter cup.
Cost to the maker↓Chicory is much cheaper than coffee.↓Robusta costs about half as much as arabica.

The price gap is why both mixtures tempt fraud: a pack labelled "100 % arabica" or "pure coffee" can quietly carry the cheaper ingredient.

When a blend becomes adulteration

In India, roasted coffee-chicory powder must contain at least 51 % coffee, so no more than 49 % chicory, and every pack must declare both percentages (IS 3802, FSSAI advisory of November 2021). When FSSAI checked 42 companies in 2021, only 13 complied with the labelling rules.

Seeing chemistry: hyperspectral imaging and the coffee spectrum

A camera that records a spectrum at every pixel

An ordinary camera records three colours per pixel. A hyperspectral camera records hundreds of narrow wavelength bands, so every pixel carries its own spectrum: how much light the material reflects at each wavelength. Stacked together, the image becomes a data cube with two spatial dimensions and one spectral one.

This project uses a HySpex SWIR-384: 384 pixels across, 288 bands from about 950 to 2500 nm, in the short-wave infrared. It is a push-broom camera: it records one line at a time while the samples move underneath, building the image line by line.

A white Spectralon strip at the start of every scan reflects almost all light at every wavelength. Dividing each pixel by the white strip in the same column turns the raw signal into reflectance, which no longer depends on the lamps or the camera's sensitivity.

Why short-wave infrared sees chemistry

Light between 1000 and 2500 nm makes chemical bonds vibrate. Each bond (C–H in oils, O–H in water and sugars, N–H in proteins and caffeine) absorbs at its own set of wavelengths, called overtones and combination bands. Where a compound absorbs, the reflectance dips. So the depth of a dip tracks how much of that compound is present, roughly in proportion once reflectance R is turned into absorbance, log₁₀(1/R). That proportionality is why simple straight-line models (PLSR) measure chicory so well.

Why HSI is a good way to measure chicory

  • ChemistryA strong, specific signal. Coffee is oily and chicory is not: the 2310 nm oil band measures 0.095 in pure arabica, 0.085 in pure robusta and only 0.024 in pure chicory. Every percent of chicory makes the dips a little shallower.
  • PixelsThousands of spectra per dish. A single powder grain is either coffee or chicory, so one pixel says little. HSI averages 12 × 12-pixel patches (about 9 mm) across the whole dish, so the result reflects the bulk blend, not one grain.
  • MapsIt shows where, not just how much. Because every patch gets its own value, the app can map chicory across a dish and show whether a blend is evenly mixed.
  • PracticalFast and non-destructive. No chemicals and no extraction; a dish is scanned in seconds and can be measured again.
  • TestedAbout 1 % error on unseen days. In this project the models were tested on whole scanning sessions they never saw, the strictest test the data allows.
  • LimitsArabica and robusta differ far less. Their spectra are close (a little more oil in arabica, more caffeine and phenolics in robusta), so the arabica : robusta split is much harder than chicory. Each calibration also belongs to its camera and lamp setup.

How a dish is analysed

  1. Raw camera counts lose their dark signal, taken from the file's own header.
  2. Each pixel is divided by the Spectralon strip in the same column, giving reflectance.
  3. The dish is found by its rim; rim, shadow and glare pixels are removed.
  4. The powder is cut into 12 × 12 px patches (about 9 mm), turned into absorbance, and the least typical 10 % are dropped.
  5. A classifier (PCA-LDA, 95 % correct on unseen days) picks the mixture; that mixture's model gives the chicory %.

Limits

  • RangeCalibrated for 0–50 % chicory, matching the 49 % legal limit. Higher values are extrapolations.
  • SetupTrained on 99 dishes scanned on four days (1, 4, 7 and 8 Sep) with one HySpex SWIR-384, one lamp setup and one source of each ingredient.
  • BlendsThe arabica + robusta + chicory model assumes those blends hold chicory at the stated level; the lab notes should confirm it.
  • StripEvery scan needs the white Spectralon strip at its start.
